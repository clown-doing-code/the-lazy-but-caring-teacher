English, by far, is one of the most popular and spoken languages around the world. As a matter of fact, it is the go-to option for people who are looking for opportunities to grow and improve their life. Jobs, education, entertainment, etc. Without a doubt, the English language has taken over many aspects of our life, becoming a standard on some of them such as business per se. Knowing that a language is intrinsically related to the culture, and having such recognition across many countries could consequently cause a loss of its identity as language in terms of culture. But the purpose of this article is not to prophesy the imminent disappearance of English, but to demonstrate the narrow relationship between language and culture, and how it is so useful when it comes to teaching, especially in controlled environments where English is being taught as a second language.
 
To frame the situation, students of the English Immersion Program; a well-known intensive program open to college students to learn the language in question, were prompted with the challenging task of carrying out a project mainly focused on the 4th of July (the independence of the United States), which I would say that is something drastically new for some of them. As a teacher in charge of the classroom, I started to document the process from day one. Two weeks of intensive hard work. Day by day students discussed and set the stage to present what is one of the most commendable projects I have witnessed so far as a teacher. Throughout those weeks I could observe how, little by little, students were being influenced positively by American culture. They went over every aspect of American culture and the 4th of July (iconic videos and songs, fashion, history, symbolic food, etc.). They did not miss a single part of it, they embraced every aspect of American culture as if they were connecting the dots on a puzzle or looking for an exit on a colorful maze. Sadly, it did not last longer than two weeks. Nevertheless, as a result of my observations, I find out how culture works as a catalyst for learning purposes, drastically increasing and fulfilling some gaps on their learning journey.  Therefore, 4th of July was not only a topic but also a window for them to see through and discover. Likewise, students did not just learn about Independence Day, they used English to culturally express ideas, question traditions, and relate them to their own life. More than a project, it worked as a tool to craft and set up new memories in their brains that will last forevermore.
 
At first, I did not plan to write about this, but they pushed me to do it. I was quite hesitant yet eager to see the result. Not because I was doubting their capabilities, but because I did not know how creative they could get to be. I could see the spark in the way they delivered their ideas, chose the words, and made connections. The way of using the language in the way they did, reminded me why teaching language through culture is so powerful. Some students went beyond plain facts, they made accurate comparisons, answered questions critically, and explained cultural symbolism evidencing a superior understanding of the contents given so far. Having the opportunity to witness their natural skills bloom is a priceless gift that every teacher would love to live.
 
Activities such as the 4th of July are proof of how effective this type of project can get to be by incorporating not just language practice and grammar but more important high level  skills such as research, peer discussion and reflection. The role of the teacher is giving students the necessary tools to craft their future, but it is nonsense if they do not know how to use them. For that reason, I would say that a teacher's job is beyond grammar and vocabulary, but giving the students the space and opportunity to express themselves in special scenarios where they put their knowledge in evidence. On top of that, by adding the cultural-focused scenarios where they get to play and discover new ways in which vocabulary and grammar can be used, it's a big yet interesting and amazing way to teach that can be also used as a workaround to the monotone.
 
To my students and all students across the other shifts: thank you for your effort, your curiosity, and the heart you put into this project. Your work reminded me why I love teaching — and why culture and language are so deeply connected. This project was a reminder that when students are given meaningful, culturally rich opportunities, they rise to the occasion — and often exceed our expectations. I'm proud of what they accomplished and grateful to be part of their journey.
